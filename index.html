The core idea

Culture is like an iceberg

Most of what we notice about another culture is the small part above the waterline. The part that actually causes misunderstandings is almost always underneath.

VISIBLE

Language, food, clothing, customs, greetings — the things you'd list if someone asked "what's the culture like?"

INVISIBLE — BUT SHAPES EVERYTHING

Values about time and relationships. Attitudes to hierarchy and authority. What counts as rude or polite. How disagreement is supposed to be expressed.

Worth rememberingMost cross-cultural misunderstandings happen below the surface — not because people are being difficult, but because the rules they're following were never visible to begin with.

Three angles

Three researchers, three questions

To make sense of what's under the waterline, this module leans on three well-known frameworks. Each one asks a different question about the same underlying problem.

1

2

Treat all three as tools, not rules. Real people are always more complex than any model — including the people you'll meet in the case scenarios later.

Framework 1

Hall — high vs. low context

How much meaning is carried by the words themselves, versus everything around them — tone, silence, relationship, history?

ConceptLow-context (USA, UK, Germany, Australia)High-context (Japan, China, Thailand, Korea)
MeaningExplicit — say what you mean.Implicit — what is not said matters too.
SilenceCan feel awkward or unclear.Comfortable and meaningful — no need to rush to fill it.
"No"Said clearly and directly.Rarely direct — watch for vague answers or a change of topic.
Try this"That may be a little difficult" can be a serious warning, not a small problem. A nod may mean "I am listening," not "I agree."

Thailand sits firmly on the high-context side of this scale. Go back and reread the warm-up email with that lens — see how many high-context signals you can now spot.

Framework 2

Hofstede — what do people value?

Four dimensions that shape workplace behaviour more than most people realise, until they run into the mismatch firsthand.

Power Distance
Flat hierarchy, boss is approachable (Denmark)Seniority respected, hierarchy is clear (Thailand)
Individualism vs. Collectivism
"I" decide alone (USA, Australia)"We" decide together (Thailand)
Uncertainty Avoidance
Comfortable with ambiguity, quick decisionsNeeds clear rules, process, and time
Long-term vs. Short-term
Quick results, transactionalBuild slowly, trust before business

For the case work ahead, Power Distance and Uncertainty Avoidance matter most — they explain a lot of what looks like silence or hesitation in a meeting.

Framework 3

Meyer — how do people behave at work?

The Culture Map narrows the lens specifically to business communication — feedback, trust, and disagreement.

Evaluating
Direct negative feedback OKIndirect negative feedback preferred
Trusting
Task-based — earned through workRelationship-based — earned through time
Disagreeing
Confrontational, open debate OKAvoids confrontation, harmony preferred
Key insightJapan sits toward the right on almost every scale above. The UK and USA tend to sit toward the left. Neither position is wrong — they're reading from different scripts.
